##### Abstract

GDP-normalised territorial carbon intensity, defined as the ratio of carbon emissions to GDP for a given year, is widely used to assess national decarbonisation progress. However, this indicator can be highly sensitive to short-lived exogenous shocks that alter emissions independently of structural economic or technological change. We analyse the 1991 Gulf War oil-well fires in Kuwait, ignited during the retreat of Iraqi forces, as an illustrative case displaying the vulnerability of territorial CO2 intensity to conflict-driven disturbances. Using
national emissions data from the Global Carbon Budget and long-term GDP series from the Maddison Project Database, we quantify the magnitude and duration of the resulting anomaly
in the trajectory of Kuwait’s carbon intensity. We find a pronounced, short-lived spike in carbon intensity that is primarily driven by abnormal combustion emissions, amplified by a
contemporaneous recession. The episode showcases that war-related impacts can distort decarbonisation indicators and potentially mislead policy interpretation when territorial
metrics are used in isolation. Our results contribute to the interpretation of decarbonisation progress and the allocation of responsibility for emissions in times of polycrisis.
